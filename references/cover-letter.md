# Cover Letter Guidance

Only use this when the user asks for cover letter help. Don't proactively offer it, most users came for CV review.

## The job of a cover letter

A cover letter is **not** a prose version of the CV. The CV says *what* you've done. The cover letter says **why this role, why this company, and why you specifically**.

A reader who has the CV in hand and reads the cover letter should learn three things they couldn't get from the CV alone. Frame the letter as an answer to three questions:

1. **Why you?** One concrete story that proves you'll be effective here, not a list of qualities.
2. **Why this role and/or company?** What specifically about *this* position or *this* employer pulled you in (not "great company culture").
3. **What do you want to learn or develop?** What you're looking to grow into here, tied to what the role and company actually offer. Keep this forward-looking and specific to them, not a generic wish list. This is the one place where naming your own growth is fair, as long as it connects to what they do.

Every cover letter should answer these three. If one is missing, the letter is incomplete.

**The letter must add something the CV doesn't already say.** The recruiter has the CV in hand. If the letter just restates the same facts in prose, it's dead weight. Give them something new: the reasoning behind the career move, a project detail that didn't fit on the CV, why this specific team, a perspective the bullet points can't carry. If everything in the letter is already on the CV, cut the letter or rethink it.

If the cover letter is just "I am hardworking and excited to join your team", it's deadweight. Many recruiters skip cover letters that read like that.

## Answer the posting's stated expectations

Most job postings spell out what they want, often under a heading like **"To thrive in this role, we believe you have:"**, "What we expect", or "Qualifications". This is a gift: the employer has listed the exact criteria they'll screen against. Use the cover letter to walk through the ones that apply to the candidate and give concrete proof for each.

- Pull the expectation list from the posting.
- For each item the candidate can genuinely back, name the proof: a project, a role, a concrete outcome. "Team-first mindset" → a team project they shipped. "Willingness to learn" → the career switch itself, or a stack they picked up fast.
- Skip the ones they can't honestly claim. Don't fake a match.
- Where there's a gap between the posting's requirement and the candidate's experience (e.g. the posting wants Angular, the candidate knows React), address it head-on: name the adjacent experience and the willingness to close the gap. Recruiters read an ignored requirement as a miss; an acknowledged one reads as self-aware.

When you have both the posting and the CV, do this mapping *for* the candidate in your feedback: list the posting's stated expectations and, next to each, the specific proof from their CV they should lean on.

## Length

- 0.5 page is great
- 1 page is the absolute max
- 4 short paragraphs is a good shape

## Structure

**Paragraph 1, Why this role / why this company.** Two sentences. Specific. Not "I am applying for the X role at Y company" (the recruiter knows). Something like: "Your post about migrating the analytics pipeline to dbt caught my eye, I spent the last year doing the same migration at [past company] and the tradeoff between Airflow and dbt scheduling is exactly the kind of problem I want to be solving."

**Paragraph 2, Why you, with one story.** Pick *one* concrete example from your experience that maps directly to what this role needs. Not your whole CV summarized, one story built with the **STAR method**: Situation (the context), Task (what needed doing), Action (what *you* specifically did), Result (what changed, ideally with a number). See `star-method.md`. Don't tell the candidate to "tell it properly" or "tell it well", that's empty; name STAR and the four parts so they know exactly what to include.

**Paragraph 3, What else you bring.** One or two short specifics that the CV doesn't fully capture: a perspective, an unusual combination of skills, something you've been building on the side. Optional, skip if you don't have something genuinely additive.

**Paragraph 4, Close.** One or two sentences. "I'd love to talk about [specific thing from the job posting]. Available [timeframe]." No "Thank you for your consideration of my candidacy at this prestigious organization."

## What to avoid

- **Reciting the CV.** The recruiter has the CV. Don't paraphrase it.
- **Generic praise.** "I admire your innovative culture and commitment to excellence", true of any company; means nothing.
- **Apologetic framing.** "Although I lack experience in X, I am eager to learn." Don't lead with a weakness. If you lack X, lean on what you *do* have that maps to the same skill.
- **Listing soft skills.** "I am hardworking, motivated, and a great team player" tells the reader nothing. Show through one example.
- **Trying to be funny.** Risky. Skip unless you're genuinely good at this.
- **Making it about your needs.** "I'm looking for an opportunity to grow my career in X." Reframe around what they get, not what you want.

## For career changers

The cover letter matters more for career changers than the CV does. The CV shows the past; the cover letter explains the *direction*.

The structure shifts:

**Paragraph 1, The pivot, in one line.** Don't hide it. "After eight years in [old field], I've spent the past year building [new thing] and I'm now looking for [new role]."

**Paragraph 2, Why this transition is real.** What have you actually *done* in the new direction? Bootcamp? Projects? Shipped code? Open source? Be concrete.

**Paragraph 3, What from the old field translates.** This is where you turn the past into an asset, not a liability. A logistics coordinator moving to engineering: stakeholder management, written communication, working under pressure across time zones, navigating ambiguity. Connect these to the role's actual needs.

**Paragraph 4, Close.** Same as standard.

## Process for helping the user

When the user asks for cover letter help:

1. **Get the job posting URL.** Without it, you can't say "why this role / why this company", and that's most of the letter.
2. **Get one story.** Ask: "What's one thing from your past experience that you'd want this employer to know about?" Then push them on the details, what was the situation, what did you do, what happened.
3. **Draft together, don't ghostwrite.** Offer a structure with their content, not boilerplate. The cover letter has to sound like them. Generic AI-written cover letters are a known recruiter pet peeve.
4. **Cut.** First drafts are always too long. Aim for half a page.
